Causal Robustness Frameworks

A cheat sheet for stress-testing causal claims. Unified example: Does exposure to a political ad change voting intention in a synthetic population?

Running Example

You run a synthetic population through Aaru. Some agents are exposed to a political ad; others are not. You observe a shift in voting intention among the exposed group. The estimated Average Treatment Effect (ATE) is +8 percentage points. But is this causal? Every framework below attacks this claim from a different angle.

1 E-values

What it tests
How strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to fully explain away your observed effect. Produces a single number: the minimum strength of confounding (on the risk-ratio scale) required to reduce the effect to null.
When to use
After any observational estimate. Quick, assumption-light sensitivity check. Works with risk ratios, odds ratios, hazard ratios, or standardized mean differences.
What it can't tell you
Whether such a confounder actually exists. A large E-value means the effect is hard to explain away, not that it is definitely causal.
Ad example: Your ATE of +8pp corresponds to a risk ratio of ~1.5. The E-value is 2.4, meaning an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both ad exposure and voting intention by a factor of at least 2.4 each. If the strongest known confounder (e.g., party affiliation) only has a factor of 1.8, your estimate looks robust.

2 Rosenbaum Sensitivity Bounds

What it tests
In a matched observational study, how much hidden bias (expressed as odds ratio Γ) would be needed to alter the study's significance. You increase Γ from 1 upward until the p-value crosses your threshold.
When to use
After propensity-score matching or stratification. Standard tool for matched designs.
What it can't tell you
The actual level of hidden bias present. Only tells you the tipping point.
Ad example: You match exposed and unexposed agents on demographics, media diet, and prior political leaning. At Γ=1.8, the p-value crosses 0.05. This means a hidden factor making agents 1.8x more likely to be exposed (and independently shifting vote intention) would nullify the result. You judge whether that is plausible in your synthetic world.

3 Negative Controls

What it tests
Whether your study design is picking up spurious associations. A negative control exposure should have no effect on the outcome; a negative control outcome should be unaffected by the treatment. If either shows an effect, something is wrong.
When to use
When you suspect residual confounding or measurement artifacts and want a quick diagnostic.
What it can't tell you
Which specific confounder is causing the problem, or how to fix it. It is a diagnostic, not a correction.
Ad example: Negative control exposure: Show agents a neutral weather report instead of the political ad. If voting intention still shifts +5pp, your pipeline has a confounding leak. Negative control outcome: Check whether ad exposure changes an unrelated behavior like breakfast preference. If it does, your model is entangling unrelated behaviors.

4 Instrumental Variables (IV)

What it tests
Estimates causal effects even with unmeasured confounders by exploiting an instrument: a variable that affects the outcome only through the treatment. Uses two-stage least squares or related estimators.
When to use
When randomization is imperfect or impossible, and you have a valid instrument (relevant, exogenous, satisfies exclusion restriction).
What it can't tell you
Whether your instrument is truly valid. The exclusion restriction is untestable. Weak instruments amplify bias.
Ad example: Instrument: random variation in ad server load causes some agents to receive the ad and others not, independent of their traits. Use ad-server assignment as instrument for actual ad viewing. The IV estimate gives you the Local Average Treatment Effect for compliers, those whose viewing was determined by the server load.

5 Regression Discontinuity (RD)

What it tests
Causal effects at a threshold. If treatment assignment jumps at a cutoff of a running variable, comparing units just above and below the cutoff approximates a local randomized experiment.
When to use
When treatment is assigned based on a score crossing a threshold. Sharp RD when assignment is deterministic; fuzzy RD when it merely changes probability.
What it can't tell you
Effects far from the cutoff. The estimate is hyper-local. Also assumes no manipulation of the running variable near the cutoff.
Ad example: Suppose agents with a media-engagement score above 70 are targeted for the ad. Compare agents scoring 69 vs 71. They are nearly identical, so any jump in voting intention at the threshold is attributable to the ad. But this only tells you the effect for agents near 70, not the full population.

6 Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

What it tests
Causal effects by comparing the change over time in a treated group versus a control group. Removes time-invariant confounders and common time trends.
When to use
When you have panel or repeated cross-section data and the parallel trends assumption is credible: absent treatment, both groups would have followed the same trajectory.
What it can't tell you
Whether parallel trends actually hold. If a concurrent shock hit only the treated group, DiD is biased. Time-varying confounders are not removed.
Ad example: Measure voting intention in two synthetic cities before and after the ad airs in City A only. City A shifts +8pp while City B shifts +2pp. DiD estimate: +6pp. But if City A also experienced a political scandal during the same period, the estimate is contaminated.

7 Placebo Tests

What it tests
Whether your estimated effect appears where it should not. Run the same analysis on a period, group, or outcome where the treatment could not have had an effect. A non-null placebo result signals model misspecification or confounding.
When to use
As a companion to DiD, RD, or any quasi-experimental method. Standard robustness check.
What it can't tell you
That your real estimate is correct. Passing placebo tests is necessary but not sufficient.
Ad example: Run your DiD analysis but pretend the ad launched one month earlier (before it actually did). If you still see a +6pp effect in this fake pre-period, your parallel-trends assumption is violated and the real estimate is suspect.

8 Manski Bounds

What it tests
The range of possible causal effects under minimal assumptions. Instead of assuming confounders are fully controlled, Manski bounds give worst-case and best-case treatment effects compatible with the observed data. Produces an interval, not a point estimate.
When to use
When you want to be honest about identification limits. Especially useful with missing data, sample selection, or when parametric assumptions are questionable.
What it can't tell you
A precise effect size. Bounds can be very wide, sometimes uninformatively so. Adding assumptions (monotonicity, monotone treatment response) tightens them.
Ad example: Suppose 20% of agents have missing voting intention data. Without assumptions about the missing data, the ATE could range from -12pp to +28pp. Adding the assumption that ad exposure never decreases voting intention (monotone response) tightens the bound to [+2pp, +14pp]. Still wide, but honestly so.

9 Falsification Tests

What it tests
Predictions your causal model makes that, if wrong, would undermine the claim. Distinct from placebo tests: falsification tests derive specific auxiliary predictions from the causal DAG and check whether they hold.
When to use
When you have a specific causal model with testable implications beyond the main effect. The more predictions that survive falsification, the stronger the model.
What it can't tell you
That the model is true. Only that it has not yet been refuted on the dimensions you tested.
Ad example: Your DAG says the ad works through emotional arousal, not information. Falsification prediction: agents who see the ad but have emotional responses disabled should show no effect. If they still shift, your mechanism is wrong (though the overall effect might still be real via a different path).

10 DAG-based Sensitivity Analysis

What it tests
Using a directed acyclic graph to systematically identify which unobserved variables could bias your estimate, and by how much. Tools like the "bias factor" or "omitted variable bias" formula let you quantify the impact of specific missing nodes in the DAG.
When to use
When you have domain knowledge to draw a plausible DAG and want to reason transparently about what could go wrong. Works well with Cinelli & Hazlett's "omitted variable bias" framework.
What it can't tell you
Whether your DAG is correct. The analysis is only as good as the graph you draw. Missing edges or wrong directions undermine everything.
Ad example: Your DAG includes media diet, age, party affiliation, and ad exposure. But you suspect "social media echo chamber intensity" is an unobserved confounder. Using Cinelli-Hazlett, you find this variable would need R-squared of 0.15 with both exposure and outcome to explain away the effect. If you believe that is implausibly high, your estimate survives.
