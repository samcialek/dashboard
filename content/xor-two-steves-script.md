# XOR and the Two Steves: Why Political Taxonomies Fail

*PRISM Audio Series — Script*

---

In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book called Perceptrons, and in it they proved something that brought neural network research to a halt for over a decade. They showed that a single-layer perceptron, the simplest possible neural network, cannot solve the XOR problem. It can learn AND. It can learn OR. But it cannot learn XOR, exclusive or, because XOR is not linearly separable. You cannot draw a single straight line through the input space that correctly classifies all four cases.

This killed an entire field of research. Funding dried up. People moved on. And it took until the mid-1980s, when Rumelhart and Hinton demonstrated backpropagation through hidden layers, for neural networks to recover. The fix was straightforward in hindsight: you add a hidden layer. That hidden layer creates an intermediate representation, a new feature space where the problem becomes linearly separable. The XOR problem was never unsolvable. It was unsolvable with the wrong architecture.

Now. Here is the political version of the same problem.

Take two people. Call them Steve One and Steve Two. Both are American. Both would describe themselves as patriots. Both, if you gave them a standard political survey, would score high on national identity, high on respect for American institutions, high on what you might loosely call civic pride. On any single-layer political taxonomy, a left-right spectrum, a two-axis compass, even something like the Pew typology, these two Steves land in roughly the same place.

But they are fundamentally different people. And the difference only becomes visible when the system is under stress.

Steve One loves America because of what it stands for. The Constitution. Freedom of speech. Rule of law. Protection of minority rights. Democratic governance. His patriotism is downstream of his ideological commitments. He is a nationalist because his nation embodies the principles he believes in. The causal arrow goes from ideology to national identity.

Steve Two loves America, full stop. The flag. The anthem. The land. His people. His ideological commitments, to the extent he articulates them, are downstream of that love. He supports the Constitution because it is American, not because of some abstract commitment to liberal democratic principles. The causal arrow goes from national identity to ideology.

Now. As long as the regime and the principles are aligned, these two Steves are indistinguishable. They vote the same way. They say the same things at barbecues. They are, from the perspective of any descriptive political model, the same person.

But the moment the regime betrays the principles, when the government starts suppressing speech, or dismantling checks on power, or targeting minority groups, the two Steves diverge completely. Steve One becomes a dissident. Steve Two becomes a fascist. Same inputs. Opposite outputs. That is the XOR problem.

And this is exactly why every major political taxonomy fails. Left-right? It cannot capture this. The political compass? Two axes are still a single-layer architecture. You cannot draw a line, or even a plane, through this input space that correctly classifies the Steves. Because the thing that distinguishes them is not a position. It is a causal direction. It is which variable is upstream of which.

And this is why Minsky and Papert's critique, which was technically correct and devastated an entire field, is the precise analogy for what has gone wrong in political science. The problem is not that people are complicated. The problem is that our models have the wrong architecture. We are trying to classify political behavior with single-layer systems, descriptive taxonomies that map observed positions, when the actual generating process requires a hidden layer. A layer that represents the causal mechanism, the direction of the arrow between identity and ideology.

This is what PRISM does. The L2 layer in the PRISM framework is that hidden layer. It does not ask where you stand on an issue. It asks why you stand there. It models the causal direction, not just the position. And once you have that hidden representation, the classification problem that was impossible with a flat taxonomy becomes trivial.

Minsky and Papert were right that perceptrons cannot solve XOR. They were wrong that this meant neural networks were a dead end. The same is true in political modeling. The flat taxonomies cannot solve the Two Steves problem. But that does not mean political behavior is unmodellable. It means we need the hidden layer. We need the causal mechanism. We need PRISM.
