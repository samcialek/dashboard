On heterogeneity: In Liu Cixin's Three Body Problem, even the alien Trisolaran civilization displays the full heterogeneity of thought — from ruthless hawks advocating humanity's destruction to individuals genuinely sympathetic to our survival. Causal Rigor

The GLP-1 question: On Squawk Box recently, the question came up — do GLP-1 drugs reduce alcohol consumption? The naive answer is yes, but the causal picture is far more interesting. There's a rebound effect: as people get more physically fit, they become more social, which can increase certain types of drinking. The real answer has loads of heterogeneity — different time-bound effects from different causal sources, operating at different scales. This is exactly the kind of question Aaru's simulation needs to get right, and exactly the kind of question that correlational methods get catastrophically wrong.

Causal rigor isn't about academic purity — it's about building simulations that survive contact with reality. When a competitor's model says "GLP-1 reduces drinking" and Aaru's model says "it depends on the archetype, the time horizon, and the social context — here's the full distribution," that's the moat.

Environment Simulation (Milieu)

I prefer "milieu" to "environment" — it captures both the macro conditions (economic climate, policy shifts, cultural moments) and the less-macro conditions any individual agent is experiencing (sleep debt, recent life events, social network dynamics). At Serif, incorporating "loads" as modifiers to causal effects and thresholds was one of our biggest breakthroughs. High sleep debt, for instance, didn't just change outcomes — it changed the threshold at which interventions became effective. The causal graph itself was load-dependent.

Scenario analysis should produce the full distribution of outcomes, not just point estimates. Different situations call for optimizing different places along the probability distribution — sometimes you want the median, sometimes the tail.

On distribution shape: I wrote about this in The Geometry of Preference — an investing perspective on why the shape of the outcome distribution matters as much as the mean. The St. Petersburg Paradox (which I first encountered at D.E. Shaw) makes this visceral: a bet with infinite expected value that no rational person would take, because the distribution shape is pathological.

The model should be fully online — recommendations updating with the latest jobs report, competitor price shifts, cultural inflection points. This makes the product sticky in a way static models can't match. The datasets should allow inferring when shifts in competitor dynamics and milieu happen, feeding a model that updates daily.

A Fully Generative Framework

The endgame is a system that climbs Pearl's causal ladder — from association to intervention to counterfactual reasoning — and stays there, updating continuously as new data arrives. Most models in production today live on the first rung: they learn associations ("people who do X tend to do Y") and stop. That's useful but brittle. Move to the second rung — intervention — and you can answer "what happens if we change X?" without actually running the experiment. Move to the third rung — counterfactuals — and you can answer "what would have happened if X had been different for this specific person, given everything else we know about them?" That's where the real value lives, and it's where almost no one operates at scale.

The goal, for partners who want it, is to turn Aaru's simulation into a fully online, self-updating prediction machine. Not a model you retrain quarterly — a system that ingests new data as it happens (a jobs report, a competitor price change, a new regulatory announcement, a cultural inflection point) and automatically understands how to interpret it. "Automatically" is doing real work in that sentence: the system needs causal structure to know which parameters a new datapoint should update and which it shouldn't. A jobs report should shift economic-sensitivity parameters, not dietary preferences. Without the causal graph, online updating is just noise propagation. With it, every new observation makes the simulation more accurate, and the product becomes something clients can't walk away from — because the model they have today is strictly better than the model they had yesterday.

The timing is right. There have been major advancements since 2022 in Bayesian VAE and autograd techniques for inferring and naming causal forces — moving from black-box latent dimensions to interpretable causal structure. Combined with online Bayesian updating, and eventually perhaps agent-based reinforcement learning for dynamic behavior, this is the path to becoming the brains of every company — a product so essential that operating without it becomes a fatal disadvantage.

A Parallel Simulator Engine (10,000+ Archetypes)

Partial pooling is extremely effective for coherent regularization, and it's one of the most underappreciated tools in simulation. Simulating a single individual gets noisy fast — you're fitting to an n-of-1. Pooling across archetypes gives you dramatically better estimates while preserving the heterogeneity that matters.

The vision: 10,000+ archetypes, each running as an autonomous agent on its own compute, with a fully online Bayesian architecture supplementing LLM outputs. When new LLM models come out, you test whether LLM-aided or non-LLM outputs perform better — and there's information in the difference itself. At Serif, we used cluster-based priors with posteriors calibrated for individuals, and that hybrid approach (starting from clusters, refining to individuals) may be ideal for Aaru as well.

Here's the mathematical argument: the joint probability distribution exists for roughly 50 metrics. With 8 levels each, that's 850 potential agent configurations — completely intractable for RL. Use a Dirichlet process to generate 10,000–20,000 clusters: this doesn't add too much bias, mitigates substantial variance, enables principled uncertainty quantification, and avoids losing information at bin boundaries. It makes the RL tractable (O(8n) collapses to a manageable space) and complements existing "ember" removal through ablation-style approaches, Bayesian hierarchical models, posterior predictive checks, and Sobol decomposition.

Redundant models predicting the same thing isn't waste — the disagreement between models is crucial for generating the true distribution before condensing to a point summary. Mode collapse, one of the persistent headaches in generative simulation, becomes much less salient when you're collapsing to 10,000 Dirichlet cluster centroids rather than trying to represent the full combinatorial space.

I understand Matt is working on internal agent consistency — partial pooling is one natural path to achieving longitudinal validation, because the archetype-level structure provides the regularization that individual-level models lack.
