# Beneath the Surface of Belief

*Six layers, five refractions, and why most attempts to model politics start too late in the chain*

March 2026 · Companion to: The Cause of Our Politics

---

Everything before explicit politics belongs to the problem of an agent encountering a world.

Before you have opinions about taxes or immigration or whether the president is doing a good job, you are a biological organism dropped into an environment you did not design. You have a nervous system that reacts to threats at a speed you didn't choose. You have a dopamine system that seeks or avoids novelty in ratios you didn't set. You have a capacity for empathy that was largely determined before you could speak.

You are, in the language of reinforcement learning, an agent. Instantiated by your own hardware, you will learn in the world and develop a *policy* -- a mapping from situations to actions. Over time, the lessons compound. What worked, what didn't, what felt right, what felt wrong. The policy crystallizes. And eventually, that policy starts generating outputs that the world recognizes as politics.

This essay is about the layers of that process -- six transformations that turn neural substrate into political behavior, and about why most attempts to model politics start too late in the chain.

Before L1, though, you are dealing with the organism's native substrate: genetics, developmental biology, and baseline neural architecture. At some point, further atomization stops being useful. The meaningful unit is often not the single neuron or the tiny mechanism but the population-level coding geometry by which the brain represents uncertainty, stimulus, and action. So the story starts with the brain's starting configuration and default response profile, not with infinitely decomposing it.


---

## L0 — Genome

The deepest layer is one you will never observe directly and cannot measure with a survey. L0 is the genome -- the raw genetic instructions that specify the broad architecture of a nervous system. Dopamine receptor density, amygdala baseline reactivity, oxytocin sensitivity, prefrontal processing speed.

But genes do not execute in a vacuum. They execute in a body, in a womb, in a developmental environment. The same genetic instructions expressed under different developmental conditions -- maternal stress, nutrition, toxin exposure, epigenetic modification -- produce different neural architectures. This is the first refraction. The genome is white light; developmental biology is the first prism. What emerges is not the genome itself but its product: a particular nervous system, wired in a particular way.

You cannot politically survey L0. It is pre-experiential. But it constrains everything that follows, because it sets the hardware on which all subsequent software will run.


---

## L1 — Neurology

L1 is what the genome and its developmental conditions produced: the actual nervous system of a living infant. This is the first useful human-level abstraction of the agent in an environment. How that neural substrate cashes out as an initial operating system for the person:

**Threat sensitivity** -- how fast and how strongly the threat-detection circuitry fires. **Novelty seeking** -- the dopamine-mediated pull toward unfamiliar stimuli. **Reward response** -- how strongly positive outcomes reinforce behavior. **Impulse control** -- the capacity to delay or override default reactions. **Social sensitivity** -- attunement to others' emotional states and social cues. **Abstraction tolerance** -- the ease of reasoning about systems rather than cases. **Baseline affect** -- the resting emotional tone, optimistic to anxious. **Attentional style** -- broad and diffuse versus narrow and focused.

These are the agent's hyperparameters. In reinforcement learning, hyperparameters define the *type of learner* an agent is -- the architecture of the network, the learning rate, the discount factor -- not what it has learned. A person high in novelty seeking will always weight new information more heavily. A person high in threat sensitivity will always discount unfamiliar options more steeply. These settings do not change meaningfully over a lifetime. They define what the system is capable of learning.

But L1 does not produce politics. It produces a *range* of possible politics, and the range is wide. A high-threat-sensitivity infant could become a cautious moderate, a hypervigilant survivalist, or a meticulous risk manager -- depending on what happens next.

What happens next is upbringing: parental socialization, religious formation, economic hardship or comfort, discrimination or privilege, community norms, media environment. This is the second refraction. L1 is the light; upbringing is the prism.

The product is not additive. It is interactive, and the interactions are many-to-many. No L1 trait maps one-to-one onto a single outcome. Defiance, for example, requires high temperamental reactance -- but also low threat sensitivity and low affiliative drive. If you are oppositional but also fearful and desperate to belong, you end up a frustrated conformist, not a rebel. The combination matters more than any single input.

The same high-threat-sensitivity infant raised in a safe suburb becomes moderately cautious -- prudent risk management, mild conservatism, a preference for the familiar. That same infant raised in a war zone becomes hypervigilant -- fortress mentality, zero-sum worldview, threat behind every stranger. Same hardware, different calibration.

The same high-social-sensitivity child raised in a diverse community develops broad compassion -- universal moral circle, instinctive concern for distant strangers. That same child raised in an insular community develops deep but bounded compassion -- intense loyalty to "your people," indifference or suspicion toward outsiders. Same mirror neurons, different training data.

## L2 — Crystallized Personality

The product of L1 refracted through upbringing is L2: crystallized personality. This is the first layer you can actually observe and measure in an adult. It is how native endowment becomes a socially legible personality through formative experience.

Family structure, attachment style, class position, religious formation, trauma or safety, schooling, peer hierarchy, and early reward structures -- these shape innate tendencies into a more stable outward way of being in the world. The high-novelty-seeker whose curiosity was rewarded becomes epistemically curious. The one whose curiosity was punished becomes a thrill-seeker instead -- seeking sensation, not ideas. Same L1 input, different L2 output, because the formative environment selected differently.

L2 is relatively stable by early adulthood. In RL terms: L1 was the architecture, upbringing was the training data, and L2 is the learned weights. You cannot re-run your childhood. By the time you are forming political opinions, L2 is baked.

But L2 is still pre-political. A person with high epistemic curiosity, strong reality calibration, and moderate everything else is not yet a technocrat or a reformer or a skeptic. They are a *type of learner* -- and what they learn next depends on the cultural circumstances they encounter as adults. Economic volatility, career trajectory, geographic mobility, marriage, parenthood, exposure to institutional failure or success, the media ecosystem they inhabit.

This is where the same innate personality profile starts to diverge dramatically. A high-order, high-discipline, high-affiliative temperament formed inside a cosmopolitan meritocratic environment produces an earnest institutionalist -- someone who loves their country through its systems, its procedures, its aspirational documents. That same temperament formed inside a locally rooted, status-threatened community produces an earnest patriot of a very different kind -- someone who loves their country through its people, its traditions, its continuity. And that same temperament formed inside a community that has experienced discrimination produces a fierce in-group advocate -- someone whose loyalty and discipline are directed toward protecting and advancing their own.

Three very different political trajectories. One underlying temperament. Three different cultural prisms.

## L3 — Political Disposition

L3 is where things finally become recognizably political. This is how general personality tendencies become specifically political orientations: order versus change, hierarchy versus leveling, institutional trust versus suspicion, boundary strength, zero-sum sensitivity, moral traditionalism, proceduralism, civic obligation, tolerance for conflict.

These are deeper than issue positions and prior to party labels. Someone can score high on public order and low on economic hierarchy. Someone can be pluralist on culture and technocratic on governance. Real politics contains these cross-currents all the time, and they are precisely what "liberal" and "conservative" erase.

PRISM measures L3 across fourteen dimensions organized into four clusters.

The **Ends** cluster captures what you want: material orientation (redistribution vs. free market), cultural defense (progressive vs. traditional), cultural uniformity (pluralist vs. assimilationist), and moral circle (universal vs. particularist).

The **Means** cluster captures how you pursue it: proceduralism (rules-bound vs. outcome-focused), epistemics (how you decide what's true), aesthetics (what kind of political leadership you find compelling), and compromise tolerance (principled vs. pragmatic).

The **Reality** cluster captures how you see the world: zero-sum orientation (positive-sum vs. zero-sum), ontology of human nature (pessimistic vs. optimistic), and ontology of the system (declining vs. thriving).

The **Self** cluster captures your political identity: partisan fusion (independent vs. partisan), tribalism (universalist vs. tribal), and engagement (apolitical vs. engaged).

Every one of these dimensions has two components: *position* (where you stand) and *salience* (how much you care). A person can be strongly progressive on cultural defense but have low salience on that dimension -- meaning they hold the view but it doesn't drive their behavior. Position without salience is an opinion. Position with salience is a motive.

L3 is durable but not permanent. The libertarian moment of 2010 and the populist moment of 2016 didn't involve mass conversions. What changed was the political-economic environment, which activated different dimensions of dispositions that had been there all along. The parochialism that was dormant became salient. The institutional trust that had been fraying finally snapped. Same people, same deep structure, different political expression -- because the environment changed which parts of the structure mattered.

## L4 — Political Ideology

L4 is where political disposition gets compressed into recognizable symbolic categories, issue bundles, and coalition identities. This is where you start calling yourself a liberal, a conservative, a libertarian, a progressive -- or where you refuse to call yourself anything, which is itself a position.

"Liberal" and "conservative" are better understood as principal components than as true dimensions. They are summary bundles of correlated views, identities, and intuitions -- not singular underlying axes that fully explain a person. Like PCA in statistics, they compress a high-dimensional disposition space into convenient labels that capture the most variance in a given era. What "liberal" captures in 1972 (Great Society, labor unions, anti-war) is not what it captures in 2024 (identity politics, credentialism, technocratic governance). Same label, different loading vector. The components rotate when the coalitional sorting changes.

This is also where the concept of **affordances** does its work. James Gibson coined the term in ecological psychology. An affordance is the interface between an agent and its environment. Not the full environment, not the full agent -- just the interface. What you can do (your active states) and what you can perceive (your sensory states).

In political terms, affordances are the choice architecture that structures political behavior: voting, protesting, donating, persuading, consuming media, organizing, opting out. These are the action possibilities that the political environment offers to an agent given its capabilities.

The affordance structure includes candidate characteristics, party platforms, coalition composition, the issue agenda, mobilization infrastructure, ballot access, and the social cost of political action. Each person's disposition determines which of these affordances they can perceive and act on. A person with high engagement and low tribalism perceives the affordance of ticket-splitting, third parties, issue-based coalitions. A person with high engagement and high tribalism doesn't see those affordances -- not because they're unintelligent but because their disposition makes those options functionally invisible. The affordance isn't there *for them*.

This layer explains why the same person votes differently in different elections without having changed. In reinforcement learning terms: L3 is the policy function, and L4 is the state space. Same policy, different states, different actions.


---

## L5 — Political Behavior

Only at the end do we get what everyone actually measures: votes, issue positions, party affiliation, candidate support, movement participation, abstention, posting, persuading, protesting, organizing, defecting, disengaging. L5 is where the person's internal structure meets institutions, incentives, laws, media, peer pressure, and available channels of action.

This is where most models start and stop. It is the action output of the agent.

The problem with modeling only L5 is the overdetermination problem. The same observable behavior -- voting for a particular candidate -- can be generated by completely different configurations at L0 through L4. Two voters with identical survey scores may have arrived there via entirely different causal paths, and their future behavior will diverge as soon as the context shifts. One is a true believer whose deep structure aligns with the candidate. The other is a contextual voter whose affordance structure happens to point the same direction this cycle. Same output, different generators, different futures.

## The Agent and the Environment

Step back and look at the full chain. There is a pattern.

At every layer, the same structure repeats: an endogenous state (something internal to the agent) is refracted through an exogenous medium (something in the environment) to produce the next layer.

| Layer | Endogenous state | Refracted through | Produces |\n|-------|-----------------|-------------------|----------|\n| L0 | Genome | Developmental conditions | Neural architecture |\n| L1 | Neurology | Upbringing | Crystallized personality |\n| L2 | Personality | Culture / lived circumstances | Political disposition |\n| L3 | Disposition | Political-economic moment | Political ideology |\n| L4 | Ideology | Political affordance structure | Political behavior |

Six layers, five refractions, the same operation applied recursively.

This is the structure of an agent learning in an environment. Reinforcement learning formalizes exactly this: an agent with a state and a policy encounters an environment with dynamics and rewards, takes actions, receives feedback, and updates. The agent's architecture is fixed (L0-L1). Its training produces a policy (L2-L3). It deploys that policy in a test environment (L4) and produces actions (L5).

But the environment isn't fixed. It goes on a random walk.

The political landscape of 2008 is fundamentally different from 2020. The affordances change. What choices are available, what's salient, what's at stake -- all of it shifts. And the agent can make different decisions at the same node depending on the context.

Consider someone who's moderately free-market. They believe markets generally work. In 2008, the affordance environment presents: should we bail out the banks? In 2020, the affordance environment presents: should we send stimulus checks? Same person, same underlying disposition, potentially different behavior. Because behavior is a function of disposition and milieu. The agent and environment are coupled.

The ideal approach for understanding political outcomes would be full agent-based simulation. Model the agents, model the environment, model the dynamics, let them co-evolve. But this requires mapping out all of reality -- every upstream cause, every interaction, every feedback loop. It is computationally intractable and epistemically impossible.

So what do we do instead?

We take advantage of a simplification. We don't need to model all of reality. We just need to model the thing that exists right beneath political behavior, even though there's significant causal depth underneath it. We can measure it if we hold all that other stuff constant by treating our measurement as a point-in-time snapshot.

## The Value Function

There's an old adage that politics is downstream from culture, and culture is downstream from personality. That's the causal chain. PRISM doesn't try to measure personality. It doesn't try to measure culture. It measures the political disposition that personality and culture have *deposited* -- the end product of all those upstream causes, frozen at the moment of measurement.

In reinforcement learning terms, this has a precise name. It's the **value function**.

The value function is the agent's learned mapping from states to values. It compresses everything the agent has learned from experience -- every interaction, every outcome, every lesson -- into a single function that says: given this situation, here's how valuable each option is to me.

The value function has a beautiful property. It is a sufficient statistic of the agent's history for predicting future behavior. You don't need to know *why* someone values economic freedom. Maybe it's personality -- dispositional openness to risk. Maybe it's formative -- grew up in a family business. Maybe it's economic conditions -- experienced real scarcity and saw markets solve it. Doesn't matter. The value function captures what all those upstream causes deposited. Condition on the current value function, and history is irrelevant for prediction.

This is exactly the Markov property. The value function IS a Markov state for political behavior.

PRISM measures the value function of a political agent.

The value function over all possible political situations would be infinite-dimensional. We need a finite characterization. This is where the 14 nodes come in.

The 14 PRISM nodes are **basis functions** of the political value function. Each one captures a dimension of variation in how people evaluate political situations. Material orientation, cultural defense, cultural uniformity, moral circle, proceduralism, epistemics, aesthetics, compromise, zero-sum orientation, ontology of human nature, ontology of the system, partisan fusion, tribalism, engagement -- together, with their positions and saliences, they form a finite-dimensional approximation of the full political value function. Not a perfect representation -- no finite basis is -- but sufficient in the statistical sense: conditional on knowing someone's 14-node profile, knowing their full biography adds no predictive power for political behavior.

This is what makes PRISM tractable. You don't need to simulate the full life history of 330 million people. You need to measure their current value functions -- their dispositions -- and those dispositions, combined with the current state of the political environment, are sufficient to predict behavior.

## Markovian and Non-Markovian

Not everything in the political system is Markovian, and the distinction matters.

L3 is Markovian with respect to L5. Given someone's political disposition, their specific biography doesn't improve your prediction of their political behavior. Two people who arrived at the same L3 profile via completely different life paths will respond to the same L4 conditions in the same way. This is the whole point of measuring disposition rather than demographics. Demographics are noisy proxies for the disposition that actually generates behavior. Disposition is the sufficient statistic.

But L3 is *not* Markovian with respect to L3 updating. When someone's disposition changes -- during a political realignment, a crisis of faith, a migration to a new context -- the *way* it changes depends on the layers below. Two people with the same L3 but different L1s and L2s will realign differently under the same pressure. The same disposition stress-tested by the same shock can fracture along different lines depending on the temperament and formative context underneath.

You have to *make* something Markovian. It doesn't come for free. The PRISM architecture earns the Markov property by choosing the right level of abstraction -- L3, the political disposition -- and defining it precisely enough (14 nodes, each with position and salience) that it captures the decision-relevant state. Choose too coarse a representation (left-right on a line), and the Markov property fails because you've thrown away information that predicts behavior. Choose too fine a representation (full biography), and the Markov property holds trivially but you've gained nothing in tractability.

The practical consequence: for behavioral prediction in steady state, L3 is sufficient. Measure the disposition, observe the environment, predict the behavior. Markov mode. For modeling realignment -- the rare but consequential moments when dispositions actually change -- you need the deeper layers. You need to know not just where someone is, but how they got there, because the path determines the fault lines.


---

For now, the point is simple. You are a stranger in a room. You have hardware you didn't choose. That hardware was calibrated by an upbringing you didn't choose. That calibration crystallized into a personality you only partially chose. That personality, encountering the cultural and economic conditions of your era, produced a set of political dispositions you experience as beliefs. Those dispositions, compressed into ideological language and filtered through the affordances of the political moment, produce the behavior the world sees and calls your politics.

Six layers. Five refractions. One agent, learning in an environment it didn't design.

The political spectrum is a projection of this process onto a line. PRISM is an attempt to map the process itself.
